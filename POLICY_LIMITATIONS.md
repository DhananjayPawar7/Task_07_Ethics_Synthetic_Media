# Policy Limitations and Residual Risk

The policy in `SYNTHETIC_MEDIA_POLICY.md` reduces risk, but it cannot guarantee that synthetic media will never be misunderstood, altered, or misused. Task 7 specifically requires an honest account of where the policy could fail.

## 1. Disclosure Can Be Removed

The policy requires multiple disclosure layers, but a downstream editor can still rename a file, cut away an opening spoken disclosure, crop a visual label, or repost an excerpt without the original caption.

This is not theoretical within the logic of my Task 6 experiment. The synthetic disclosure was strong in the intended artifact, but an excerpt beginning later in the file would not automatically contain it.

The policy reduces this risk by requiring independent disclosure on excerpts, but it cannot control every copy once content leaves university-controlled channels.

## 2. Provenance Is Not Indestructible

The policy requires preservation of original files, hashes, and available content credentials. Those records are useful only if the organization keeps them and if the relevant artifact can be compared with the approved original.

Task 6 showed that the C2PA markers visible in the original ElevenLabs MP3 did not remain visible after an ordinary re-encoding test. A technical provenance layer can therefore be weakened or lost even without malicious intent.

Internal provenance improves accountability, but it is not a universal guarantee that every downstream copy will retain machine-readable origin information.

## 3. Detection Is Probabilistic

The Task 6 detector correctly classified Attempt 2 as AI-generated with a 96% AI score and classified all 19 analyzed segments as AI-generated. That was one successful test, not proof that every detector will identify every future synthetic artifact.

Different generators, editing processes, background noise, compression, or future models may change detection performance. A false positive could also incorrectly label authentic content as synthetic.

The policy therefore treats detection as supporting evidence rather than final proof.

## 4. Consent Can Be Formally Complete but Practically Weak

A person may sign a consent form without fully understanding how synthetic representation will feel after publication or how far the media may travel. Power relationships may also make a person feel pressure to agree even when the form appears voluntary.

The policy narrows purpose, duration, distribution, and reuse, but paperwork cannot eliminate every concern about unequal power or later regret.

## 5. Reviewers Can Disagree

The policy relies on human reviewers deciding whether an artifact could confuse a reasonable audience. Different reviewers may interpret the same synthetic recording differently.

People who work with AI tools frequently may also become desensitized to synthetic cues and underestimate how a general audience will interpret a recording.

The ambiguity rule helps by requiring escalation when reviewers disagree, but judgment remains unavoidable.

## 6. Scale Creates Operational Pressure

The policy requires review for batch generation, but real organizations face deadlines, staffing shortages, and pressure to publish quickly. A well-intentioned employee may reuse an approved template in a new context and assume that prior approval still applies.

Over time, exceptions can become informal habits. Audits and incident reporting can reduce that tendency, but they cannot remove the organizational incentive to trade review for speed.

## 7. Tool Behavior Can Change

Synthetic-media products can change their models, interfaces, metadata practices, and output quality. A workflow considered low-risk at one point may later produce more realistic output or handle provenance differently.

The policy requires review when a substantially new capability is adopted, but it cannot predict every vendor change.

## 8. The Policy Cannot Govern Outsiders

An internal policy governs people acting on behalf of the athletics communications office. It cannot stop an outsider from synthesizing a coach's voice, fabricating an announcement, stripping a label, or falsely claiming that a file came from the university.

What the policy can do is make the organization's legitimate workflow more traceable. Preserved originals, hashes, consent records, and approval records give the institution stronger material for comparing an unauthorized artifact with authentic content.

## 9. Bad-Faith Employees Can Ignore Rules

A determined employee or contractor could intentionally bypass the consent process, export an unlabeled file, or publish from a personal account. No written policy can physically prevent every deliberate violation.

This reliance on good-faith compliance is a real weakness, but not a reason to abandon policy. Clear rules make violations identifiable and provide a basis for investigation and accountability. Audit, separation of creator/reviewer roles, and retained records reduce but do not eliminate this risk.

## 10. High-Consequence Boundaries Will Still Require Judgment

The policy names categories such as injury status, discipline, recruiting commitments, emergencies, and crisis communications. New situations may arise that are not explicitly listed but carry similar consequences.

Employees therefore still need judgment. The refusal rule is intentionally broader than a fixed list: when the identity and accountability of the human speaker matter materially to the audience, the office should prefer a real accountable speaker.

## Residual-Risk Conclusion

This policy cannot make synthetic representation harmless. It can make approved use more limited, more transparent, more consent-based, and easier to audit.

The strongest safeguard is not any one detector, watermark, content credential, or consent form. It is the combination of:

- refusing deceptive and high-consequence uses;
- obtaining consent before identity-based synthesis;
- disclosing synthetic media repeatedly;
- preserving internal provenance;
- requiring human review;
- controlling automation and scale;
- maintaining an incident-response process;
- acknowledging that some requests should use a real human speaker instead.

The policy succeeds if it makes responsible use easier to recognize and irresponsible use harder to normalize. It does not eliminate residual risk, and it should not create the false confidence that synthetic-media governance has been “solved.”
